Urban trees do more than make cities look attractive. Their leaves provide shade, which can lower surface temperatures during hot weather. Trees also release water vapor through a process called transpiration, helping cool the surrounding air. In neighborhoods with little vegetation, roads and buildings absorb solar energy and release it as heat, creating what researchers call the urban heat island effect.

However, simply planting more trees does not guarantee equal benefits. Young trees provide less shade than mature trees, and different species vary in their ability to survive drought, pollution, and limited soil. Maintenance also matters. Without regular watering and care, newly planted trees may die before they provide significant cooling.

For these reasons, many urban planners now focus not only on the number of trees planted but also on where they are planted, which species are selected, and whether long-term maintenance is funded. A successful urban forestry program therefore requires both environmental knowledge and sustained public investment.
